# Comparative Historical Design

## Route by inferential aim

| Aim | Design | Central question |
|---|---|---|
| Test a specified mechanism | theory-testing process tracing | Did the proposed mechanism operate? |
| Discover a transferable mechanism | theory-building process tracing | What links cause and outcome? |
| Explain mechanism failure | theory-revision | Where did the process break and which condition was missing? |
| Explain a singular outcome | explaining-outcome | What minimally sufficient causal package best accounts for it? |
| Compare trajectories | comparative historical analysis | Which differences in conditions, sequence, or mechanisms explain divergence? |
| Build or repair a concept | concept formation | What attributes and boundaries capture the historical variation? |
| Combine population and process evidence | nested design | Which broad pattern selects cases and which process evidence explains it? |

Do not force a generic literature review, descriptive history, or correlation-only problem into process tracing.

## Structured-focused comparison

Literature anchors: METH-CASE-001, METH-CASE-002, METH-CASE-003, METH-CHA-001, CN-METH-001, and CN-DEB-002; read their roles and limits in [Method-Evidence Map](method-evidence-map.md) before using the label.

Use one common question set across cases:

- How is the outcome defined and timed?
- Which causes and background conditions were present?
- Which alternatives were historically available?
- What was the sequence of decisions and activities?
- Which mechanism fingerprints should appear?
- Which rivals predict the same or different evidence?
- What evidence exists at a comparable access level?
- What conclusion ceiling applies to each case?

The comparison is structured because it asks the same questions and focused because it collects only evidence relevant to the theoretical problem.

## Within-case and cross-case inference

Cross-case comparison identifies patterned differences and scope. Within-case analysis tests productive links and sequence. Neither substitutes for the other:

- covariance does not establish a mechanism;
- a mechanism in one case does not establish prevalence or average effect;
- multiple narratives do not create a comparison without common concepts and questions.

## Temporal comparison

Cases can be countries, provinces, organizations, policy episodes, decision rounds, or periods. A within-unit temporal comparison can be stronger than a cross-national comparison when measurement and source access are more equivalent.

Guard against spillover between periods and against defining one long process as independent cases.

## Mixed and nested methods

Quantitative, text-as-data, network, formal, or geospatial methods can:

- discover distributions and anomalies;
- define a case universe;
- measure timing or exposure;
- test population-level associations;
- select typical or deviant cases.

Historical materials can then evaluate concepts, sequence, entities, activities, and evidence-generating processes. Do not use a quantitative association as proof of an unobserved mechanism, or a vivid process account as proof of population prevalence.

## Design memo

Every comparative design records:

- inferential target;
- case universe and inclusion rules;
- outcome threshold;
- focal and rival explanations;
- selection strategy;
- comparability and independence;
- time windows and lags;
- evidence-access symmetry;
- within-case protocol;
- external-validity limit;
- negative or shadow comparison;
- conclusion ceiling.
