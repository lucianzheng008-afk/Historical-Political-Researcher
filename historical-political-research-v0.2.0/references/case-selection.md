# Case Selection

## Start with the universe

Literature anchors: METH-CASE-001, METH-CASE-003, METH-CHA-001, and METH-AGENDA-001; read their roles and limits in [Method-Evidence Map](method-evidence-map.md) before using the label.

Define the case universe before naming focal cases. Record:

- unit of analysis and level of analysis;
- temporal and spatial bounds;
- inclusion and exclusion rules;
- outcome definition, threshold, and observation date;
- plausible positive, negative, and ambiguous cases;
- source-access differences across the universe.

If the universe cannot be described, the selection claim cannot be audited. A famous or convenient episode may still be studied, but it must be presented as an explaining-outcome case rather than as a representative test.

## Match strategy to inference

| Strategy | Use when | Main risk | Required defense |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical case | showing how an established relationship works in an ordinary setting | treating typicality as self-evident | population benchmark and explicit similarity rule |
| Deviant case | revising a theory that predicts poorly for a case | choosing on an interesting residual after seeing the mechanism | pre-specified prediction and residual criterion |
| Most-likely case | a theory should perform especially well | success is weak confirmation | explain why the case is genuinely most likely and what failure would mean |
| Least-likely case | success would be surprising under background conditions | hidden favorable conditions | specify the adverse conditions and rival predictions |
| Crucial case | rival theories make discriminating predictions | calling any important case crucial | list the rival predictions and decisive observations in advance |
| Most-similar comparison | cases share many background conditions but differ on cause or outcome | unmeasured differences and post-treatment matching | justify similarity and trace the proposed difference |
| Most-different comparison | different settings share a cause and outcome | overclaiming necessity or sufficiency | enumerate alternative common factors and scope limits |
| Within-unit temporal comparison | the same unit changes across periods | period spillover and endogenous breakpoints | independent periodization and transmission audit |
| Shadow comparison | a lower-intensity comparison checks selection or scope | asymmetric evidence depth | state its limited role and use common questions |

These labels describe inferential roles, not intrinsic properties of cases.

## Selection-on-the-outcome audit

When all focal cases display the outcome:

1. identify outcome-absent cases in the universe;
2. check whether the proposed cause also appears without the outcome;
3. add at least one negative or shadow case when feasible;
4. if negative cases are inaccessible, downgrade claims about necessity, sufficiency, and prevalence;
5. distinguish explaining a chosen outcome from estimating a general causal effect.

Never infer that a mechanism is generally sufficient from successful cases alone.

## Evidence-access symmetry

Comparison requires comparable opportunities to observe evidence. For each case record:

- archive openness and survival;
- language and digitization;
- elite versus non-elite visibility;
- public-relations or retrospective incentives;
- number and independence of source families;
- whether absence could be generated by different record-keeping systems.

Unequal access does not automatically invalidate a design, but it changes the conclusion ceiling. Do not interpret a richly documented case as more causal merely because it is more visible.

## Independence and interference

Ask whether cases influenced one another through diffusion, coercion, learning, shared institutions, common shocks, or researcher-defined period boundaries. If so, model or narrate the connection rather than treating observations as independent replications.

## Required case-selection memo

Use `assets/templates/case-selection-memo.md` and `assets/templates/case-universe.csv`. The memo must include:

- the inferential target;
- the universe and exclusions;
- case-selection strategy;
- positive, negative, ambiguous, and shadow cases;
- expected evidence under the focal and rival explanations;
- comparability, independence, and access asymmetry;
- feasible alternatives that were rejected;
- the maximum claim the selected cases can support.
