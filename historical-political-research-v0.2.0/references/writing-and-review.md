# Writing and Review

## Write the title last

A defensible title follows a stabilized question, contribution, cases, and time boundary. Prefer one of three forms:

1. **Puzzle-led:** `Why X despite Y? Evidence from Cases/Period`
2. **Mechanism-led:** `Mechanism and Outcome: A Comparative Historical Analysis of Cases/Period`
3. **Theory-revision-led:** `Beyond/Bounding Theory T: Mechanism or Historical Condition in Cases/Period`

Avoid titles that promise only a perspective, topic, significance, enlightenment, or broad historical review. Do not claim causality, comparison, generality, or a “new theory” beyond the actual design.

If a user requests titles before the question is ready, first produce a minimum question contract. Any illustrative title must be marked `PLACEHOLDER` and paired one-to-one with the unverified assumption on which it depends. Do not let a plausible title silently choose the outcome, mechanism, case, or finding.

## Analysis-unit paragraph

Build each empirical paragraph in this order:

1. analytical judgment linked to a claim ID;
2. precise evidence with source status;
3. mechanism update: what entity acted, through what activity, changing what state;
4. rival assessment;
5. bounded implication and uncertainty.

Chronology may organize evidence, but chronology is not the explanation. If a paragraph can be summarized only as “then this happened,” rewrite it around a causal or conceptual judgment.

## Full manuscript architecture

A default historical-political-science paper contains:

1. introduction: empirical anomaly, theoretical stakes, question, answer preview, contribution, and design;
2. existing explanations: strongest rival families and the discriminating gap, not a catalogue;
3. concepts and theory: definitions, mechanism, scope, rivals, and observable implications;
4. research design: universe, selection, periodization, sources, comparison protocol, and limitations;
5. empirical analysis: common questions across cases or periods, with claim-evidence links;
6. comparison and updating: similarities, differences, rival performance, and revised scope;
7. conclusion: answer, contribution, limits, and implications without new evidence.

For short proposals, compress sections but retain the inferential chain.

## Contribution language

Choose the smallest warranted verb:

- clarifies or distinguishes a concept;
- identifies an anomaly;
- documents a sequence;
- uncovers or supports a mechanism;
- bounds, differentiates, revises, or rejects a theory;
- extends a mechanism to a specified scope;
- develops a new proposition or framework.

Do not upgrade “illustrates” to “proves,” one case to a general law, or local originality to field-wide novelty without a completed search.

## Review protocol

Literature anchors: METH-PT-001, METH-CASE-001, METH-MEASURE-001, METH-SOURCE-001, METH-SOURCE-002, and METH-SOURCE-003; read their roles and limits in [Method-Evidence Map](method-evidence-map.md) before using the label.

Review in two passes.

### Constructive pass

- reconstruct the strongest version of the paper's question, argument, and contribution;
- identify what is genuinely promising;
- distinguish repairable presentation problems from design failures;
- propose the smallest change that would materially strengthen inference.

### Adversarial pass

- state the best rival explanation;
- look for selection on the outcome and missing negative cases;
- test whether concepts are circular, stretched, or defined by the result;
- challenge period boundaries, continuity carriers, and path-dependence claims;
- trace source dependence and evidence-generating processes;
- compare claims against the actual evidence ceiling;
- mark normative conclusions presented as empirical findings;
- flag fabricated, unverifiable, or citation-mismatched material.

## Review output

For every issue report:

- severity: blocking, major, or minor;
- verdict: `PASS`, `PARTIAL`, or `FAIL`;
- location or claim ID;
- diagnosis;
- why it matters for inference;
- concrete repair;
- verification test.

End with separate judgments on puzzle, theory, concepts, design, evidence, historical analysis, comparison, contribution, writing, and venue compliance. A paper can be promising yet not submission-ready.
