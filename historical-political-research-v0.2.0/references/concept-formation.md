# Historical Concept Formation

## Goal

Build concepts that capture historical experience without collapsing actor meanings into contemporary analytical language or creating flattering local synonyms for existing ideas.

## Two vocabularies

Always separate:

- **actor language:** terms used by historical participants in their setting;
- **analytical language:** categories used by the researcher to compare and explain.

A modern analytical concept may be useful for a historical case, but disclose the translation and do not claim it was the actors' self-understanding.

## Concept record

For every `CON-*`, specify:

- core attributes and intension;
- extension and included cases;
- threshold for membership;
- dimensions if the concept is graded;
- neighboring concepts and discriminating differences;
- positive, negative, and boundary cases;
- level of analysis;
- period-specific meaning changes;
- observable indicators;
- scope conditions;
- risk of normative loading or conceptual stretching.

Record the measurement chain explicitly:

```text
background concept -> systematized concept -> dimensions and attribute relations
-> observable indicators -> coding or classification rule
```

For every indicator, state whether it is a constitutive attribute, a cause, or an outcome. An outcome cannot serve as the sole indicator of the concept later used to explain it. Record convergent and discriminant validity checks where the materials permit them.

## New-concept gate

A new concept is justified only when it:

1. identifies empirical residue that existing concepts misdescribe;
2. has explicit attributes and boundaries;
3. differs from its closest neighbors;
4. classifies negative and borderline cases coherently;
5. produces observable implications;
6. travels beyond the original case or states why it should not;
7. creates explanatory or descriptive gain rather than local renaming.

Normatively positive labels are not analytical concepts without exclusion rules.

## Across-time comparison

Before comparing a concept across periods, test:

- semantic equivalence;
- institutional function;
- actors and membership;
- authority and enforcement;
- rights, duties, and resources;
- relation to the wider political order.

Shared names do not establish equivalence. Shared functions do not establish genealogy. Different names may mask comparable mechanisms.

## Avoid circularity

Do not infer a concept from the outcome and then use the concept to explain the same outcome. For example, do not define state capacity by successful policy implementation and then claim that capacity explains success. Use independent indicators and disconfirming cases.

## Theory discrimination through concepts

Literature anchors: METH-CONCEPT-001, METH-CONCEPT-002, METH-MEASURE-001, and CN-FIELD-002; read their roles and limits in [Method-Evidence Map](method-evidence-map.md) before using the label.

When an established concept appears historically local:

1. reconstruct its original cases and purposes;
2. identify hidden assumptions;
3. test whether those assumptions hold in the new case;
4. distinguish measurement failure from theoretical failure;
5. revise the concept minimally before inventing a replacement;
6. compare explanatory gain and loss.

The history of a concept can expose scope limits, but author identity or political position does not itself invalidate a theoretical proposition.
